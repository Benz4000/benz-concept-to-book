# Reader feedback without losing the book's intent

Use this reference when actual beta, editorial, cultural, accessibility, or other reader feedback is supplied or its review is requested. Do not contact, recruit, send material to, or imply endorsement by readers without authorization for that action. Drafting a request does not authorize sending it.

## Record what the feedback establishes

Add feedback to the existing issue register. Preserve the original report or a faithful linked paraphrase, with only identity information needed for the task.

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| Feedback ID and origin | Actual supplied report, firsthand user report, historical report, or clearly labeled simulation; date when known |
| Reviewed artifact | Book/edition, version or hash if known, and exact passage/page; unknown version stays unknown |
| Reader context | Relevant audience familiarity or role when supplied; do not infer credentials or demographic identity |
| Coverage | What the reader actually read and any stated limits; do not infer a full read from one comment |
| Observation | What the reader experienced, misunderstood, expected, or found effective |
| Proposed remedy | The reader's suggestion, kept distinct from the observation |
| Goal and protected qualities | Applicable Foundation decision, intended effect, and successful qualities to retain |
| Disposition and evidence | Adopt, adapt, investigate, defer, or decline, with reason, affected work, and verification needed |

Unknown-version feedback can identify a useful hypothesis. Compare its cited passage with current material before applying it or calling it current-version evidence. Keep secondhand reports and historical use labeled. A simulated reader exercise is a hypothesis generator, not actual audience validation, cultural consultation, or an independent beta review; report its assumptions and tested scope.

## Triage the experience before choosing the remedy

1. Locate the reported passage and compare it with the current Foundation and artifact. Separate an observable continuity/factual problem, orientation problem, intended discomfort or ambiguity, audience mismatch, and personal taste. A reader's experience is real evidence of that response; the proposed fix may still be unsuitable.
2. Look for underlying causes and corroborating evidence. Several independent readers encountering the same obstacle may justify wider inspection. Repeated copies of one report are not independent corroboration, and majority preference does not automatically override the book's purpose.
3. Check conflicts against scoped goals and existing priorities. If one reader wants earlier explanation and another values mystery, determine the minimum information needed to follow the action while preserving the intended reveal. If goals cannot resolve a material choice, present the concrete tradeoff and continue unrelated work.
4. Choose the smallest sufficient response. State what should improve and what must survive: agency, emotional effect, voice, cultural distinction, pacing, ambiguity, humor, or source boundaries as relevant. Declining or adapting a suggestion requires a reason, not dismissal of the reader.
5. Repair and inspect before/after in context. Update dependent summaries, source/canon records, artwork, or layout when affected. Recheck both the reported problem and protected qualities; avoid solving clarity by destroying the intended experience.
6. Record the resulting disposition and coverage. A local repair checked by the agent is not confirmed reader satisfaction. If a later actual response arrives, attach it to the reviewed revision. Otherwise state that reader revalidation is pending or was not requested.

Separate blocking defects from optional improvements using [quality and priorities](quality-and-priorities.md). A supplied comment does not automatically commission new chapters, a new art campaign, or a comprehensive rewrite. User-authorized new direction can change the Foundation; record it as such.

**Illustrative conflict, not canon:** one reader cannot tell why a character refuses help, while another praises the restrained dialogue. Inspect whether the action supplies the needed motivation. A small consequential action may resolve the first concern while retaining the dialogue; another explanation paragraph is only one possible remedy. The example establishes no universal preference for indirect exposition.
